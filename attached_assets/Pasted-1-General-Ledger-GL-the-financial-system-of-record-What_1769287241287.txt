1) General Ledger (GL) — the financial system of record

What it should do

Own the Chart of Accounts (CoA) and accounting rules: funds, segments (entity/department/program/project/location), natural accounts, cost centers, activities.

Support multi-entity (ministries/units/branches), multi-fund, multi-currency, and (if needed) consolidation and inter-entity eliminations.

Run the period close process: accruals, allocations, reclassifications, reversals, adjustments, closing calendar, close checklists.

Provide journal management with approvals, supporting documents, recurring journals, and strong audit trail.

Enforce posting controls (valid segment combinations, posting periods, tolerance rules, suspense handling).

Best-practice outcomes

Every financial transaction (AP, AR, procurement, assets, payroll interface) posts to the GL with a consistent CoA and full traceability.

2) Accounts Payable (AP) — liabilities and supplier payments

What it should do

Vendor invoice capture (manual, e-invoice, OCR optional), 3-way match (PO–GRN–Invoice) where procurement applies.

Payment processing: payment runs, approval steps, payment methods (bank transfer, checks), withholding rules, remittances.

Vendor statement reconciliation, credit notes, debit notes, disputes, duplicate invoice detection.

Integrate with commitment control and budget availability checks before liabilities and payments are approved.

Best-practice outcomes

No payment without authorization, budget availability, and full audit trail from requisition → PO → receipt → invoice → payment → GL.

3) Accounts Receivable (AR) — billing, collections, and customer accounts

What it should do

Customer master data, invoicing/billing, receipts, allocations, credit management, dunning/collections.

Support multiple revenue streams and channels (POS, online payments, bank deposits), plus automated matching.

Recognize revenue correctly (cash vs accrual basis) and support revenue reconciliation to bank.

Best-practice outcomes

Clean linkage between billed amounts, collected cash, and revenue recognition; minimal “unidentified receipts.”

4) Treasury & Cash Management — liquidity, bank, and payments control

What it should do

Bank account management, bank reconciliation (automated import + matching rules), cash positioning.

Cash forecasting, cash concentration (where applicable), signatory controls, payment security.

Manage payment files, bank connectivity, and controls for release/confirmation.

Government best-practice extras (if relevant)

Treasury Single Account (TSA) concepts, cash pooling, warrant releases, and centralized cash visibility.

5) Budgeting & Forecasting — planning + budget execution discipline

What it should do

Budget formulation: ceilings, proposals, review cycles, scenarios, MTEF (if used), versioning.

Budget execution controls: appropriations, allotments, virements, supplementary budgets; carryovers.

Real-time comparison: budget vs commitments vs actuals.

Forecasting: rolling forecasts, cash forecasts, and scenario planning tied to actuals.

Best-practice outcomes

The budget is not a spreadsheet on the side—FMIS enforces it at transaction time.

6) Procurement & Expense Management — spend governance and P2P execution

What it should do

Procurement workflows: requisitions, approvals, sourcing/RFQs (optional), POs, receipts (GRN), supplier performance basics.

Expense management: travel, per diem, advances, reimbursements, mileage, receipt capture, policy enforcement.

Contract-linked purchasing and spend tracking against contracts/frameworks.

Best-practice outcomes

Clear separation between “procurement spend” and “employee expense spend,” but both controlled and posted correctly.

7) Financial Reporting & Analytics — statutory + management reporting

What it should do

Standard statements (trial balance, balance sheet, income statement/cash basis reports), plus budget execution reports.

Configurable report builder with row-level security and segment filters.

A reporting layer/data mart for dashboards without impacting transaction performance.

Audit-ready outputs: drill-down from statements → journal → source transaction → attached evidence.

8) Security, Compliance & Integration — platform controls (non-negotiable)

What it should do

Role-based access with segregation of duties (SoD), maker-checker approvals, delegation, and strong audit logs.

Identity: SSO/MFA, access reviews, account lifecycle, privileged access controls.

Compliance controls: immutable logs, retention policies, exception reporting, configuration change tracking.

Integration: APIs/webhooks, ETL, file-based imports where needed, integration monitoring and retries.

What’s missing to meet best international practices

Your list is strong, but most “international-grade” FMIS implementations add these because they close critical control gaps:

A) Commitment Control (Funds Control / Budget Availability Control)

Why it matters: This is the backbone of disciplined budget execution.

Reserve budget at requisition/PO, check availability at invoice/payment, prevent overspend, manage multi-stage commitments.

B) Fixed Assets Management

Asset register, capitalization rules, depreciation, revaluation/disposal, tagging, physical verification, asset lifecycle.
Without this: balance sheet integrity suffers; audits flag missing/incorrect asset records.

C) Inventory & Stores (if you manage goods)

Stock receipts/issues, warehouses, reorder points, valuation, stock counts, consumption posting to GL.
Even if minimal: government/health/education often need it.

D) Contract Management (especially for government and large enterprises)

Contract registry, milestones, deliverables, variations, retention, performance securities, expiry alerts, spend against contract.
Without this: procurement-to-payment traceability breaks on long-running agreements.

E) Project/Program/Grant Accounting

Project budgets, funding sources, donor restrictions, cost allocation, reporting by program/project.
Critical for donors and public investment programs.

F) Revenue Management (beyond AR)

If revenue is complex (fees, licenses, levies), you often need:

tariff schedules, assessments, exemptions, penalties/interest, receipting controls, reconciliation.
Otherwise AR is too generic.

G) Debt & Investment Management (Treasury depth)

Debt schedules, servicing, guarantees, on-lending, investment instruments, compliance reporting.
(Highly relevant for finance ministries/large entities.)

H) Close & Financial Controls Workspace

Close calendar, checklists, auto-reconciliations, exception queues, approval of adjustments, close sign-off.
This is how you operationalize reliable reporting.

I) Master Data Management (MDM) + Data Governance

Supplier/customer master governance, CoA governance, validation rules, duplicate prevention, change approvals.
Most FMIS failures are master-data failures.

J) Document Management & E-Signatures (evidence-by-design)

Attachments to every transaction, versioning, retention, and optional e-sign for approvals/contracts.
Audit readiness improves dramatically.

K) Payment Gateway / Digital Payments (where applicable)

Integration with banks, mobile money, card processors; reconciliation and settlement controls.

L) Risk, Internal Controls & Audit Toolkit

SoD conflict reports, continuous controls monitoring, audit sampling exports, anomaly detection, exception dashboards.

A best-practice FMIS “module map” you can use

If you want the “complete” internationally credible structure:

Core Ledger & Close (GL, COA, period close, consolidation)

Budget Formulation + Budget Execution / Commitment Control

Procure-to-Pay (supplier mgmt, contracts, procurement, AP, payments)

Order-to-Cash / Revenue (billing/assessments, AR, collections)

Treasury (cash, bank, reconciliation, forecasting, debt/investments if needed)

Assets + Inventory (as applicable)

Projects/Grants/Programs (if applicable)

Reporting/BI + Audit (drill-down, statutory + management reporting)

Platform (security/SoD, workflows, integration, document management, monitoring)
